## Origin Classification Schema

Each question is classified into one of three primary pedagogical lineages:
1. **Standard Textbook Classic:** Canonical problems drawn directly from standard international textbooks on probability, statistics, and machine learning (e.g., Sheldon Ross, Bertsekas & Tsitsiklis, Hogg & Craig, Bishop).
2. **Adapted Academic Problem:** Classic problems originating from top university coursework (e.g., UC Berkeley CS 188, MIT 6.041, Stanford CS 229, UIUC ECE 313) or historical mathematical paradoxes (e.g., Chevalier de Méré, Bertrand's box, Mosteller).

### Question 3: Counting 4-digit Numbers Divisible by 3 from $\{1, 3, 4, 6, 7\}$
* **Origin Classification:** GATE Exclusive / Indian National Olympiad & Quantitative Aptitude Classic.
* **Primary Reference Textbook:** *Higher Algebra* by H.S. Hall and S.R. Knight; *Quantitative Aptitude for Competitive Examinations* by R.S. Aggarwal.
* **Key Theorist / Mathematician:** **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (Father of Modular Arithmetic; *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*, 1801).
* **Academic Lineage & Pedagogy:** Divisibility by 3 is fundamentally a modular congruence property ($N \equiv \sum_{i=0}^k d_i \pmod 3$). Since the total sum of the five digits is $21 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, forming a 4-digit subset without repetition requires omitting a digit $d \equiv 0 \pmod 3$ (either $3$ or $6$). The problem tests modular partition analysis combined with permutation factorials ($2 \times 4! = 48$).

### Question 12: Independent Coin Tosses and Mutually Exclusive Events ($T \cap S$)
* **Origin Classification:** Standard Textbook Classic.
* **Primary Reference Textbook:** *Introduction to Probability* by Dimitri P. Bertsekas and John N. Tsitsiklis (Chapter 1, Section 1.2: Probabilistic Models).
* **Key Theorist / Mathematician:** **Andrey Kolmogorov** (*Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung*, 1933; formalizer of the axiomatic foundations of modern probability).
* **Academic Lineage & Pedagogy:** Tests comprehension of mutually exclusive events: in $n=3$ tosses, having $\ge 2$ heads ($H \ge 2$) and $\ge 2$ tails ($T \ge 2 \implies H \le 1$) cannot happen simultaneously. The event intersection is empty ($T \cap S = \emptyset$), yielding probability zero.

### Question 20: Parameter Count for Naïve Bayes with $K$ Binary Features
* **Origin Classification:** Academic Coursework Classic.
* **Primary Reference Textbook:** *Machine Learning* by Tom M. Mitchell (McGraw-Hill, 1997, Chapter 6: Bayesian Learning, Section 6.9); *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (PRML)* by Christopher M. Bishop (Springer, 2006).
* **Key Theorist / Mathematician:** **Reverend Thomas Bayes** (1763) and **Tom M. Mitchell** (Carnegie Mellon University).
* **Academic Lineage & Pedagogy:** Standard qualifying examination question at Carnegie Mellon (CMU 10-701) and Stanford (CS 229). It illustrates how the Naïve Bayes conditional independence assumption collapses the exponential parameter space from $2(2^K - 1) + 1$ down to linear complexity: $1$ class prior $+ 2K$ class-conditional parameters $= 2K + 1$.

### Question 24: Bayesian Network Factorization and Conditional Independence
* **Origin Classification:** Academic Benchmark / Graphical Models Foundation.
* **Primary Reference Textbook:** *Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems: Networks of Plausible Inference* by Judea Pearl (Morgan Kaufmann, 1988); *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach (AIMA)* by Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig (Chapter 14).
* **Key Theorist / Mathematician:** **Judea Pearl** (Turing Award Winner 2011; creator of Bayesian networks, d-separation, and causal calculus).
* **Academic Lineage & Pedagogy:** Directly tests Pearl's core concept of **v-structures / colliders** ($U \to W \leftarrow V$). Two independent causes $U$ and $V$ become conditionally dependent once their shared effect $W$ is observed ("explaining away"), making the statement "$U$ and $V$ are conditionally independent given $W$" false.

### Question 36: Expected Throws Until Two Consecutive Even Numbers
* **Origin Classification:** Academic Stochastic Processes Classic.
* **Primary Reference Textbook:** *Introduction to Probability Models* (10th/11th Editions) by Sheldon M. Ross (Academic Press, Chapter 4: Markov Chains, Section on Mean First Passage Times and Patterns); *Introduction to Probability* by Bertsekas & Tsitsiklis.
* **Key Theorist / Mathematician:** **Andrey Markov** (inventor of Markov chains, 1906).
* **Academic Lineage & Pedagogy:** A canonical stochastic waiting-time problem. It utilizes first-step analysis (conditioning on the outcome of the next transition) to set up a linear system of boundary equations: $E_0 = 1 + \frac{1}{2}E_1 + \frac{1}{2}E_0$ and $E_1 = 1 + \frac{1}{2}E_0$, yielding $E_0 = 6$.

### Question 57: Exponential Distribution Parameter Relationship ($5 E[X] = \text{Var}(X)$)

* **Key Theorist / Mathematician:** **Agner Krarup Erlang** (Danish engineer; pioneer of tele-traffic queueing theory).

### Sample Question 7: Fair Coin Flipped Twice Given at Least One Tail ($P(TT) = 1/3$)
* **Origin Classification:** Historical Probability Classic / Bertrand Paradox Variant.
* **Primary Reference Textbook:** *Fifty Challenging Problems in Probability with Solutions* by Frederick Mosteller (Addison-Wesley, 1965, Problem 13: "The Three Cards"); *A First Course in Probability* by Sheldon Ross.

* **Academic Lineage & Pedagogy:** One of the most famous counterintuitive problems in mathematical education. Conditioned on $A = \{HT, TH, TT\}$, the sample space shrinks to 3 equally likely outcomes, giving $P(TT | A) = 1/3$ instead of $1/4$ or $1/2$.

### Sample Question 9: Subset Conditioning Inequality ($B \subset A \implies P(B|A) \ge P(B)$)

### Sample Question 45: Voting Transition Probabilities with Habitual and Random Voters
